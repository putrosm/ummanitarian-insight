Four Days to Sanctions, Forty-Seven Years Without: Two Speeds at the Security Council

Iraq was sanctioned four days after invading Kuwait and faced authorisation of force in under four months. Resolutions on Israeli settlements have stood since 1979 with no enforcement attached.

By Ummanitarian Insight Editorial Team · November 2026 · 4 min read · Law & Politics

The Security Council chamber at UN Headquarters in New York, photographed in September 2024. Photo: Jdforrester / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.

A meme circulating this week contrasts two men and two sets of United Nations resolutions: Saddam Hussein, it says, breached two resolutions and was removed by force, while Israeli prime ministers have breached far more and face no penalty. The claim is political, but the underlying question can be answered with dates. What the Security Council records is not one standard applied twice. It is two speeds.

Iraq invaded Kuwait on 2 August 1990. The Council demanded withdrawal the same day in resolution 660. Four days later, on 6 August, resolution 661 imposed a comprehensive sanctions regime: an embargo on trade, an assets freeze, a ban on financial transfers. On 29 November 1990, one hundred and nineteen days after the invasion, resolution 678 authorised member states to use "all necessary means" to restore international peace and security. Sanctions in four days, force authorisation in under four months.

The parallel record on Israeli settlements begins earlier and ends differently. Resolution 446, adopted on 22 March 1979, determined that settlements in the occupied territories had no legal validity. Resolution 465, adopted unanimously on 1 March 1980, called on Israel to dismantle existing settlements and halt new ones. Resolution 2334, adopted on 23 December 2016 by 14 votes to none, restated that settlements violate international law. All three were adopted without sanctions, without an enforcement mechanism, and without any authorisation of force. From resolution 446 to today is forty-seven years.

The Gaza file follows the same instrument. Resolution 2712, on 15 November 2023, called for humanitarian pauses; it passed with 12 votes while Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States abstained. Resolution 2720, on 22 December 2023, demanded the opening of all crossings for aid and the appointment of a senior humanitarian coordinator. Then, on 17 November 2025, resolution 2803 gave effect to the peace plan agreed by Israel and Hamas in October 2025. Adopted with 13 votes in favour while China and Russia abstained, it endorses the plan, welcomes a Board of Peace, authorises the deployment of an International Stabilization Force and allows a National Committee to administer Gaza. It was welcomed by the Palestinian Authority and denounced by Hamas. The text contains no sanctions and no authorisation for the use of force.

The consequences are counted elsewhere. OCHA reported that between the October 2025 ceasefire agreement and 9 November 2026, Gaza's health ministry recorded 1,613 Palestinians killed and 5,833 injured. Heavy rain from 6 November flooded shelters used by an estimated 226,000 people. Around 42 per cent of essential medicines and 55 per cent of chemotherapy medicines were at zero stock in October. In the West Bank, demolitions displaced 150 Palestinians in a single week and 410 settler attacks linked to the olive harvest were recorded since 1 September.

The difference between the two speeds is procedural rather than moral, and it is visible in the instruments. Sanctions and force authorisation require a Chapter VII finding and the concurrence of the five permanent members. A resolution that is allowed to pass by abstention can carry a demand without any enforcement clause attached. That arithmetic, not the number of violations, is what separates the Iraq file from the settlements file.

Which leaves the question the meme does not ask. Enforcement is available in this system; it is granted selectively. The measurable tests ahead are narrow: whether resolution 2803's stabilisation force receives authority to act without consent, whether arms transfers are conditioned anywhere, and whether the International Criminal Court's warrants produce cooperation or countermeasures. Until one of those changes, the record will keep producing resolutions that read the same and behave differently.

Ummanitarian Perspective

The useful question is not whether the system is hypocritical, which is an argument, but whether enforcement is predictable, which is a measurement. On the record above, the same council can produce sanctions in four days and a demand with no enforcement clause in forty-seven years.

For humanitarian planning, that difference is operational. A resolution that compels shapes access, inspection regimes and liability. A resolution that requests shapes press releases. Agencies plan against the first and absorb the second, which is why OCHA's shelter and medicine figures keep moving in the wrong direction while the legal file grows thicker.

Three watch points would show whether the instrument changes: the authority granted to the stabilisation force under resolution 2803, any conditionality attached to arms transfers by member states, and whether International Criminal Court warrants are met with cooperation or with countermeasures. Each is measurable. None requires a meme.
