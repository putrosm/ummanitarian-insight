595 Households, 14 Organisations: What Cyclone Maila’s Survivors Say About the Response

Three months after Category 5 Cyclone Maila struck Solomon Islands’ Western and Choiseul Provinces, the Pacific Humanitarian Team has published something rarer than a situation report: a systematic comparison of what 14 responding organisations say about their accountability systems — and what 595 displaced households say they actually experienced.

By Ummanitarian Insight Editorial Team · July 2026 · 3 min read · Field Ops

Coastal stilt villages near Auki, Malaita, Solomon Islands — the settlement pattern found across the archipelago, where homes sit metres from the waterline and a Category 5 system leaves little room for error. Photo: DFAT Australia via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0.

Cyclone Maila formed in the Solomon Sea in early April and did something forecasters rarely see: it developed, intensified to Category 5 and weakened again without ever leaving that stretch of water. Australia’s Bureau of Meteorology recorded ten-minute sustained winds of 215 km/h, gusts near 295 km/h and a central pressure of 924 hPa, noting Maila may be the strongest system on record for the Solomon Sea. Around 150,000 people were affected across Western and Choiseul Provinces, where a State of Disaster was declared.

The first Pacific Humanitarian Team situation report, issued on 17 April, sketched the immediate toll: one confirmed death in Wagina and seven people missing in Marovo, 59 schools damaged — six of them severely, affecting roughly 2,000 pupils — more than 31,000 people in need of emergency shelter, and nine health clinics reported damaged and non-functional. A suspected diarrhoeal outbreak was already being tracked on Babanga and Simbo Islands.

By early May, the emergency had settled into something slower and harder. The third situation report described widespread displacement into evacuation centres, schools, churches and host communities; rising protection risks for women, children and persons with disabilities; and a hazard specific to these islands — unexploded ordnance shifted and exposed by flooding and erosion, endangering both residents and responders. Displacement assessments across 13 communities in Western Province had by then identified 844 displaced individuals, while livelihoods and food security continued to deteriorate as crops were lost and incomes disrupted.

Then, in July, came the report that makes Maila worth revisiting. The Pacific Humanitarian Team’s Accountability to Affected People analysis set two datasets side by side: surveys of 14 humanitarian organisations about their own engagement and feedback systems, and community-level assessments of 595 displaced households in Western Province. On paper, the machinery exists. The organisations report foundational two-way communication channels and complaint mechanisms in place — hotlines, community meetings, suggestion channels, the standard architecture of post-disaster engagement.

The households tell a different story. The analysis found significant gaps in community awareness that those systems exist at all, inconsistent follow-through once complaints are made, and the persistent exclusion of marginalised groups — persons with disabilities and people of diverse SOGIESC among them — from both information and feedback loops.

That distance between the two datasets is the finding. An accountability mechanism that affected people do not know about is not a mechanism; it is a line in an agency’s self-assessment. Maila’s response scaled up quickly by Pacific standards — national coordination activated, provincial operations centres running, assessments feeding response plans. What the July report measures is the part that dashboards do not: whether the system worked from the side of the people it was built for.

Indonesian Summary

Siklon Maila (Kategori 5, awal April 2026) kemungkinan sistem terkuat yang pernah tercatat di Laut Solomon — sekitar 150.000 orang terdampak di Provinsi Western dan Choiseul, dengan 31.000 lebih membutuhkan hunian darurat, 59 sekolah rusak, dan sembilan klinik kesehatan tidak berfungsi. Tiga bulan kemudian, laporan Accountability to Affected People dari Pacific Humanitarian Team membandingkan klaim 14 organisasi kemanusiaan dengan pengalaman 595 rumah tangga pengungsi di Provinsi Western. Hasilnya: mekanisme umpan balik memang ada di atas kertas, tetapi banyak warga tidak tahu keberadaannya, tindak lanjut keluhan tidak konsisten, dan kelompok marjinal — termasuk penyandang disabilitas — tetap tersisih. Jarak antara laporan organisasi dan pengalaman warga itulah temuan utamanya.

Ummanitarian Perspective

Feedback mechanisms are the easiest box in humanitarian response to tick and the hardest to make real. The Maila analysis is valuable precisely because it refuses to take the tick at face value: it asks the 595 households, not the 14 agencies, whether accountability happened. The pattern it surfaces — systems that exist but are unknown, complaints that enter but never return, disabled and marginalised people outside the loop entirely — recurs across responses far larger than this one. The corrective is not another mechanism. It is measuring every mechanism from the user’s side, as this report does, and treating an unanswered complaint as a response failure rather than a communications footnote. In an archipelago that will meet its next Category 5 sooner than anyone would like, that discipline is not optional.
