Twelve Hours, Twelve Rivers: Why Are 136 Clinics Still Short of Staff?

Fifteen health workers walked twelve hours through twelve rivers in Tembagapura. The district blamed a rotation schedule, the video blamed empty shelves. The staffing standard explains both.

By Ummanitarian Insight Editorial Team · November 2026 · 4 min read · Health & Displacement

Map: Ummanitarian Insight, from OpenStreetMap data. Tsinga is served by one grass airstrip and footpaths; no road is mapped within 4.5 kilometres of the village.

On 11 June 2026 a video lasting one minute and thirty-nine seconds began circulating from the highlands of Mimika. In it, fifteen health workers walk for twelve hours from Ainggogin to Banti in Tembagapura district, crossing twelve rivers without bridges. One of them is reported to be running a high fever. The post was read as proof of abandonment: medicine finished, test kits finished, no aircraft, and only their feet left.

The district government answered within days, and its account differs on almost every point. According to the explanation reported by New Guinea Kurir on 11 June, the group walked because of a duty rotation and because one officer had to attend basic training in Timika. Hundreds of rapid diagnostic test kits were in stock, and no record showed medicine or test kits running out. The officer with fever had contracted malaria in lowland Timika before deployment, not in the highlands, which the regent described as not endemic. The walk itself, the report says, was a decision taken at clinic level before approval from the health office.

What both accounts accept is the geography. There is no road into the valley. The airstrip is the door. On 16 May 2025 a landslide cut access between villages and destroyed residents' gardens, and 2.5 tonnes of food had to be flown in on a chartered aircraft while Rp150 million was channelled through the social service, according to ANTARA's report of 7 June 2025.

The disease burden is not in dispute either. Mimika's malaria centre recorded 1,389,784 people tested and 144,110 positive cases between January and 10 November 2026, across 81 health facilities. A study of the district's programme data shows testing rising from 189,243 in 2020 to 1,191,218 in 2025, while the positivity rate fell from 41.1 per cent to 15.6 per cent. More testing, fewer positives per test, and still a caseload measured in six figures.

The staffing arithmetic is where the two versions of the story meet. Central Papua province has 148 public clinics, and only 12 of them, 9.5 per cent, hold the full set of nine health worker types required by the national standard, according to the provincial health office. The shortfall is 447 people: 55 general practitioners, 115 dentists, 74 nutritionists, 65 environmental health officers, 62 pharmacists, 55 laboratory technologists, 32 health promoters and 15 midwives. Tembagapura district itself holds 11,453 residents across 2,606 square kilometres, about four people per square kilometre.

Chart: Ummanitarian Insight. Figures from the Central Papua provincial health office, reported by ANTARA in 2026. Dentists account for the largest single vacancy category, 115 of 447.

The province has named the mechanism behind that. Extreme geography and low population density produce small capitation claims, small facility income, and difficulty keeping staff in the interior, which drives an exodus to towns. Its proposed remedy is a certification-based professional allowance paid from the national budget, modelled on the allowance that already exists for teachers. Whether that is funded will decide staffing more than any single flight of supplies.

Which leaves the video as a proxy argument. The claims that can actually be tested are narrower and duller: whether each clinic meets the nine-worker standard, whether stock records are open, whether rotation is scheduled or improvised, and whether the airstrip is usable in the weeks that matter. On the ground, the difference between abandonment and improvisation is often a rotation roster that nobody published.

Ummanitarian Perspective

Both versions of the story are useful, and neither is complete. The viral account locates the failure in supplies, which produces a shipment. The government account locates it in a scheduling decision, which produces a memo. Neither addresses the number that explains the recurring pattern: 136 of 148 clinics in the province do not hold the full team the national standard requires.

A clinic that is short of a pharmacist, a laboratory technologist and a nutritionist cannot be fixed by an aircraft. It can be fixed by a posting decision and a pay formula that makes the interior survivable. That is why the provincial proposal matters more than the next relief flight: an allowance tied to certification, paid centrally, changes who is willing to stay. Low density is not a temporary condition in the highlands, so a funding formula based on capitation will keep producing the same vacancies.

There is a verification lesson too. A one-minute video and a three-day rebuttal cannot both be taken at face value, and the reflex to defend either side delays the only question that can be answered with documents: were the shelves empty, and by whose record. Publishing stock registers and rotation rosters would settle in a week what a year of argument has not.
