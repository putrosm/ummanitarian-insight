Nine Hundred Attacks, 333,470 Patients: Who Is Protecting the People Who Do the Treating?

On the association's 76th anniversary, Indonesian doctors counted smoke-related patients while the WHO counted attacks on health care worldwide. The two records share a single question.

By Ummanitarian Insight Editorial Team · October 2026 · 5 min read · Health & Displacement

Illustration: Ummanitarian Insight. Figures from WHO surveillance reported on 14 August 2026 and from the Ministry of Health count released on 22 October 2026.

Indonesia's medical association turned seventy-six on 24 October 2026, and the anniversary press conference at its Jakarta secretariat was dominated by smoke from land and forest fires, which had pushed respiratory cases past a third of a million people in seven provinces. In August, the World Health Organization had reported a different kind of pressure on the same profession: more than 900 attacks on health care worldwide in the first eight months of the year.

The local record first. According to figures released through the national disaster agency on 22 October, health facilities recorded 333,470 accumulated cases of acute respiratory infection, known in Indonesia as ISPA, in fire-affected areas between 18 August and 21 October 2026. Of that total, 258,298 were patients aged five and over and 75,172 were children under five. “The spread of recorded ISPA cases covers 68 districts and cities in seven provinces,” said Then Suyanti, the ministry's director of environmental health, in the release. She linked the steep rise to thickening haze.

IDI chair Slamet Budiarto told reporters at the secretariat that prolonged exposure can produce respiratory and lung infection and, over the long term, cancer, citing air quality in affected cities including Palembang as unhealthy to hazardous. No significant reports of illness among health workers had reached the association, he said, and doctors were continuing to see patients in masks. The Indonesian Private Hospitals Association reported outpatient respiratory visits rising, with some hospitals distributing multivitamins to their own staff so that service could continue.

The global record is kept by the same agency that counts pandemics. On 14 August, the World Health Organization said it had recorded more than 10,400 attacks on health care across 29 countries and territories since 2018, resulting in about 5,700 deaths and more than 8,000 injuries, and that no one had been held accountable. Between January and August 2026 it counted more than 900 attacks, with at least 900 deaths and more than 1,400 injuries, a rate above four a day. Most occurred in Ukraine, Lebanon and the occupied Palestinian territory.

WHO documents the consequences as service loss rather than outrage. A study of 69 attacks in northwest Syria found that outpatient consultations fell by 51 per cent the following day, with the reduction lasting up to 37 days. In Sudan, 37 per cent of assessed facilities were reported non-functional in 2026; in Gaza all 36 hospitals have been damaged, with half remaining partially functional. The agency also recorded 44 incidents of abduction, arrest or detention of health workers and patients, mostly detention of staff, affecting 94 people.

Set side by side, the two records describe one problem with two causes. The person treating patients absorbs a risk that institutions were meant to carry. In Indonesia this season the risk arrives as air, and belongs to land clearing, drainage and enforcement. Abroad it arrives as a weapon, and belongs to armies and armed groups. The mechanism of harm differs; the accounting looks the same, because in both cases the loss surfaces later, as a clinic that cannot see the next patient, or a doctor who does not return to work.

The anniversary produced no ceremony worth reporting. It produced two questions instead. Whether the district air readings behind those 333,470 cases will be published beside the case counts while the season is still running. And whether the ten-thousandth attack on a hospital will produce the first prosecution.

Ummanitarian Perspective

Protection of health care is one of the few obligations in international humanitarian law that almost every state has accepted in writing, and one of the few that almost no state has enforced. The record now runs to five figures of attacks and a prosecution count of zero. That gap is not a mystery of jurisprudence; it is a decision about what gets investigated, and by whom. An attack on a hospital that is never adjudicated does not stay unpunished so much as unrecorded, which is the more durable outcome.

The domestic version of the same gap is quieter and easier to fix. Smoke harm is counted after the fact, in clinics, by staff working in masks, while the air readings that would allow a school to close or a family to keep a child home are not published beside the case count on the same day. Pairing the two records would not stop the fires, but it would move part of the risk off the person treating patients and back onto the institutions that decide land use. A season counted that way would show whether the 75,172 children were reached early or late, which is the only measure of the year that survives the greetings.
