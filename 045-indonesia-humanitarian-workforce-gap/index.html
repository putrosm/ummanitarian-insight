Four Per Cent Funded, One Team Certified: Why Is Local Capacity Still Rented?

The humanitarian system is contracting and local organisations absorb most of the loss. Indonesia holds the coordination chair, but its workforce is still paid project by project.

By Ummanitarian Insight Editorial Team · November 2026 · 4 min read · Capacity & Resources

Illustration: Ummanitarian Insight. The first figure is ALNAP's 2025 funding share to local and national actors; the second is the WHO classification of the Muhammadiyah emergency medical team, announced in August 2025.

In 2025 the humanitarian system lost about nine per cent of its organisations, and more than 12,000 staff had been let go by April. Most of the organisations that closed were local and national ones, and their staff lost jobs at roughly twice the rate of the workforce overall. According to ALNAP's State of the Humanitarian System 2026, that is what a funding contraction looks like when it lands on the layer that holds the response together.

The shape of the system makes this predictable. By 2024 humanitarian action had grown to 5,600 organisations and about 835,000 staff, 80,000 more than in 2021. Local and national NGOs and community organisations accounted for 81 per cent of entities, and 94 per cent of humanitarian staff were nationals of the country where they worked. Money and authority did not follow the same curve. Local and national actors received 4.3 per cent of humanitarian funding directly in 2025, 8.7 per cent including indirect funding, and held 11 per cent of Humanitarian Country Team seats.

Employment conditions show the same skew. Only 20 per cent of frontline workers had permanent contracts, and 19 per cent had no formal contract at all, against 94 per cent of international NGO staff with contracts. In Ukraine, international UN staff earned 17 times more than national staff of local NGOs. Practitioners also reported that local knowledge was often used for data rather than for decisions: only 13.6 per cent said a new needs assessment drove prioritisation in 2025.

The Indonesian register

Indonesia sits at the stronger end of that picture, at least on paper. On 19 August 2025 the United Nations-led Humanitarian Country Team was replaced by the Indonesia Humanitarian Coordination Platform, co-chaired by national civil society and OCHA, with about 80 member organisations by May 2026. In August 2025 the Muhammadiyah Emergency Medical Team became the first Indonesian team classified by the World Health Organization, the 16th worldwide, after a process that began in 2017. Seven other Indonesian teams are working toward the same classification.

The workforce behind those achievements is thinner than the achievements suggest. Indonesia's response model depends on volunteers and ad hoc secondment rather than on a standing roster of trained coordinators, and the rules that govern volunteers are still being revised: the national disaster agency opened its updated volunteer regulation to public consultation in February 2026. Organisational capacity is the other constraint. A United States-funded programme with Humanitarian Forum Indonesia trained member organisations on project financial management and donor compliance and produced a joint fundraising code of ethics, work that indicates where the baseline sits. A UNICEF-led assessment of water and sanitation in emergencies found response quality sub-optimal and coordination duplicated.

Community health cadres at a neighbourhood health post in 2021. Posyandu work is mostly unpaid and rarely covered by accident insurance. Photo: Moh. Aripin / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

The people doing the work carry the risk. A 2024 study for the Inter-Agency Standing Committee found that fewer than half of workers in local and national NGOs had access to evacuation plans, accident insurance, security infrastructure or risk monitoring, and that contracted international staff were far better covered.

Put together, the two registers describe a squeeze rather than a gap. Funding falls, staff are cut, and the skills that were being built through project money lose the project that paid for them. Localisation without multi-year institutional funding does not build capacity; it rents it, and the rental ends when the grant does. Three numbers will show whether Indonesia's gains survive: the share of funding reaching national actors directly, the contract status of frontline staff, and how many of the seven emergency medical teams reach classification.

Indonesian Summary

Pada 2025 sistem kemanusiaan kehilangan sekitar sembilan persen organisasinya, dan lebih dari 12.000 staf telah dirumahkan hingga April. Sebagian besar yang tutup adalah organisasi lokal dan nasional, dan staf mereka kehilangan pekerjaan sekitar dua kali lebih cepat daripada rata-rata pekerja kemanusiaan. Padahal pada 2024 sistem ini tumbuh menjadi 5.600 organisasi dengan sekitar 835.000 staf, 81 persen di antaranya organisasi lokal, nasional, dan berbasis komunitas, dan 94 persen stafnya adalah warga negara tempat mereka bekerja. Uang dan kewenangan tidak mengikuti kurva yang sama: aktor lokal dan nasional hanya menerima 4,3 persen dana kemanusiaan secara langsung pada 2025, 8,7 persen bila termasuk tidak langsung, dan memegang 11 persen kursi tim kemanusiaan negara. Hanya 20 persen pekerja garis depan berstatus kontrak tetap, dan 19 persen tanpa kontrak formal sama sekali. Indonesia berada di sisi yang lebih kuat dalam hal koordinasi: platform koordinasi nasional menggantikan tim bentukan PBB sejak 19 Agustus 2025 dengan sekitar 80 anggota per Mei 2026, dan tim medis darurat Muhammadiyah menjadi tim Indonesia pertama yang terklasifikasi WHO pada Agustus 2025, ke-16 di dunia, sementara tujuh tim lain masih menuju klasifikasi. Namun tenaga kerjanya lebih tipis daripada capaian itu: respons bergantung pada relawan dan peminjaman staf, aturan relawan baru diuji publik pada Februari 2026, kapasitas administrasi hibah masih harus dilatih, dan kurang dari separuh pekerja di organisasi lokal punya akses rencana evakuasi, asuransi kecelakaan, atau dukungan keamanan.

Ummanitarian Perspective

The interesting question is not whether local actors can deliver, since the evidence says they do, but what kind of organisation survives the next contraction. An organisation funded project by project builds a team, then loses it between grants; an organisation with institutional funding keeps a roster, pays insurance and trains replacements. Those two look identical in a proposal and behave differently in the third week of a response.

Indonesia's coordination gain is real and unusual: a national platform with 80 members and civil society in the chair is ahead of most countries. But coordination is the cheapest part of localisation to hand over, because it requires meetings, not payroll. The expensive parts, multi-year core funding, insurance, certification and career paths, remain with the international layer that is itself contracting.

The practical implication for national organisations is to read funding proposals for what they build and not only for what they deliver. A grant that pays for activities and leaves no roster, no certified staff and no reserve has bought a response, not capacity. The same test applies to Indonesia's seven emergency medical teams in the classification pipeline: the value is not the certificate but the standing team that the certificate forces an institution to maintain.
