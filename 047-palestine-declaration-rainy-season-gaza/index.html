Forty Thousand Shelters, Thirty-Eight Years: What Does the Declaration Mean in a Flooded Tent?

The PLO declared independence in Algiers on 15 November 1988. Thirty-eight years on, the first heavy rains after the ceasefire flooded the shelters of more than 226,000 people in Gaza.

By Ummanitarian Insight Editorial Team · November 2026 · 4 min read · Law & Politics

Flooded tents in Deir el-Balah, Gaza Strip, January 2025. Photo: Ashraf Amra / UNRWA via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0 IGO.

On 15 November 1988, in Algiers, Yasser Arafat read a declaration before the Palestine National Council that proclaimed a state over the West Bank, Gaza and East Jerusalem. The text, drafted by the poet Mahmoud Darwish, has been read as a founding document ever since. On 14 November 2026, marking its thirty-eighth anniversary, President Mahmoud Abbas said the declaration reaffirmed the Palestinian right to freedom, sovereignty and statehood with East Jerusalem as capital, according to the official news agency WAFA.

Nine days earlier, the rain arrived. Heavy rainfall from 6 November flooded tents, shelters and service points across the Gaza Strip. By 9 November the Site Management Cluster had logged about 473 alerts covering more than 46,000 households, or roughly 226,000 people who may have been affected, including more than 5,000 households reportedly sleeping outside any shelter. Reported damage covered over 40,000 family shelters, of which more than 9,000 were destroyed beyond repair. A further 960 service delivery points were damaged, according to OCHA's situation report of 13 November 2026.

Shelter kits funded by the United Kingdom and UNICEF, staged in Gaza on 1 December 2025. Nearly all of the Strip's 2.1 million residents live in tents or makeshift shelters. Photo: FCDO / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.

The water did not stay outside. Sewage-contaminated stormwater now poses a public health risk, compounded by flooded health facilities, suspended services and medicine shortages. In October, about 42 per cent of all essential medicines, 55 per cent of chemotherapy medicines and 73 per cent of supplies for open-heart surgery and cardiac catheterisation were at zero-stock levels. The WASH Cluster found 85 per cent of assessed households in moderate to severe water insecurity, and 66 per cent with access to less than the minimum six litres of drinking water per person per day.

Crowding in wet tents has produced a measurable signal. Between 27 September and 31 October, health partners recorded 294 probable viral and 119 probable bacterial meningitis cases, 68 per cent of them in Gaza governorate. Of 202 cases individually documented through 5 November, 77 per cent were infants under one year old. The Health Cluster attributes the rise to crowded tents and schools, deteriorating shelter and sanitation, and exposure to unsafe water.

Relief is arriving more slowly than it did a week earlier. Between 2 and 8 November, 262 UN and partner trucks carrying supplies were manifested for entry; 228 were offloaded on the Israeli side of Kerem Shalom, a fall of about 42 per cent compared with the previous week. The drop is attributed mainly to the suspension of the Egyptian corridor between 3 and 8 November, and to reduced volumes available to partners amid funding constraints.

The casualty count has not stopped either. Gaza's Ministry of Health reported 22 Palestinians killed and 90 injured between 3 and 9 November, bringing the total since the October 2025 ceasefire agreement to 1,613 fatalities and 5,833 injuries. In the same week, 119 patients, including 40 children, were medically evacuated through Rafah and Kerem Shalom. In the West Bank, demolitions displaced 150 Palestinians in a single week, settler attacks linked to the olive harvest reached 410 since 1 September, and education-related incidents nearly doubled in the third quarter, affecting 149 schools and close to 28,000 students and education personnel.

The funding gap sits behind all of it. The 2026 humanitarian appeal for the occupied Palestinian territory seeks just over 4 billion dollars to reach 3.6 million people across Gaza and the West Bank. Thirty-eight years after a declaration that claimed sovereignty over the same territory, the practical question in Gaza this month is narrower: whether a family sleeping outside a shelter can be moved into one before the next storm, and whether the meningitis curve turns down before winter ends.

Ummanitarian Perspective

The anniversary is a legal claim; the tents are an operational one. Thirty-eight years after the Algiers declaration, the test of statehood in Gaza is being measured in shelters, litres and medicine stock, not in diplomatic recognition.

Three numbers from the past week should drive planning rather than rhetoric. More than 5,000 households still sleep outside any structure, which makes every further storm a health event. Forty-two per cent of essential medicines at zero stock means the system is running on contingency, not capacity. And a meningitis curve concentrated in infants under one year is the kind of signal that turns a shelter problem into a mortality problem within weeks.

For humanitarian actors, the practical ask is unglamorous: shelter-grade materials before the next front, a functioning corridor into Gaza, and reagents and paediatric fluids that are not traded against chemotherapy supplies. Declarations are remembered on a calendar. Winter is not.
