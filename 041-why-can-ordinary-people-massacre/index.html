Why Can Ordinary People Massacre and Still Feel Righteous?

A Venice prize-winning documentary on Israeli targeting has reached Indonesian cinemas. Its most uncomfortable evidence is not a death toll but the vocabulary that lets ordinary men keep working.

By Ummanitarian Insight Editorial Team · October 2026 · 5 min read · Law & Politics

Tel Aviv at night. The interviews in NAZA were recorded on rooftops in the city over three years. Photo: Chenspec / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0 (2015).

NAZA, the documentary by Yuval Abraham and Rachel Szor, opened in Indonesian cinemas on 16 October 2026 through KlikFilm, after winning the Special Jury Prize at the 83rd Venice International Film Festival. Its evidence is not a death toll. It is a vocabulary, described by 24 Israeli soldiers and intelligence personnel interviewed at night on rooftops in Tel Aviv over three years, with faces shown as silhouettes and voices altered.

The title is the first exhibit. According to the film, NAZA is the Israeli military acronym for nezek agavi, Hebrew for collateral damage, used as a number attached to a potential target: the expected count of civilians killed if the target is struck. An interviewee quoted in the film states the grammar of it plainly: “We say 15 NAZA. We don’t say 15 people killed and certainly not that 15 children and women will be killed.” According to testimony presented in the film, values below 20 required no special authorisation, while higher values could be approved, up to 500 in one case. Interviewees also describe an artificial intelligence system called Lavender, which one of them calls “a factory of targets”, and a practice of checking a child’s phone to establish when the father is at home, referred to in the film as “Where’s Daddy?”.

None of that framing is new to the study of atrocity. In 1973, Herbert Kelman described three conditions under which violence proceeds without moral restraint: authorisation, by which responsibility moves upward; routinisation, by which killing becomes ordinary work; and dehumanisation, by which victims leave the moral community. Albert Bandura catalogued the linguistic half of the mechanism in 1999, listing euphemistic labelling among the ways people disengage from their own conduct. Zygmunt Bauman argued in 1989 that bureaucratic distance, rather than sadism, made the Holocaust administratively possible. Nick Haslam and Steve Loughnan showed in 2014 that dehumanisation rarely needs to be total: denying a group depth of feeling is often enough. These studies were written about other places and other decades. They describe a mechanism, not a verdict on any person who appears in this film.

Israel rejects the film’s account. The IDF Spokesperson’s Unit said it relies on anonymous testimonies “from individuals whose identities cannot be verified, including whether they served in the IDF, in what capacity, or whether they were involved in the matters about which the allegations are being made”. The military said claims about national and military strategy were “clearly beyond the knowledge of junior-level soldiers”, and that decisions on the selection and approval of strikes “are made by human personnel only, in accordance with IDF guidelines, and not by artificial intelligence”.

The consequence of a word like NAZA is documentary before it is moral. Armed forces keep records, and the record fixes what an institution knows about itself. A file that carries an expected civilian figure, rather than a list of expected dead, produces statistics that are internally consistent and externally unreadable. It also relocates disagreement: where the account of a strike exists as a number, contesting it requires the log, the approval chain and the message traffic, not only the graves. That is why the dispute over this film turns on the verification of anonymous testimony, the point the military raises and the point still unresolved.

NAZA has been sold into more than 50 territories. The Guardian, which produced it with JW Films, has said it will stream the film free on its website after the theatrical run.

Bukti utamanya bukan jumlah korban, melainkan kosakata: 24 tentara dan perwira intelijen Israel yang diwawancarai malam hari di atap gedung Tel Aviv selama tiga tahun, dengan wajah disamarkan dan suara diubah. Menurut film, NAZA adalah akronim militer Israel untuk nezek agavi, istilah kerusakan sampingan, yang dipakai sebagai angka perkiraan warga sipil yang akan tewas bila sasaran diserang. Salah satu narasumber menyatakan, “kami menyebut 15 NAZA, bukan 15 orang tewas”. Kesaksian lain menyebut sistem kecerdasan artifisial bernama Lavender dan praktik memeriksa telepon anak untuk memastikan ayahnya sedang di rumah. Juru bicara IDF menolak isi film karena bersandar pada kesaksian anonim yang identitas dan perannya tidak dapat diverifikasi, dan menegaskan keputusan pengeboman hanya diambil personel manusia, bukan kecerdasan artifisial. Penelitian psikologi sosial tentang otorisasi, rutinisasi, dan dehumanisasi menjelaskan mekanisme umum di balik bahasa semacam itu, bukan kesimpulan hukum atas peristiwa tertentu.

Ummanitarian Perspective

Two habits make this story easy to misread. The first treats bureaucratic cruelty as a property of one nation, which converts a portable mechanism into a fixed character and leaves every other institution unexamined. The second treats it as unfathomable, which is a way of not looking. The historical record points elsewhere: Browning’s Reserve Police Battalion 101 and Mamdani’s account of Rwanda describe men who were not monsters before they became perpetrators, and structures in which refusal was possible but costly. Language is the cheapest of those structures to install and the hardest to see, because it arrives as professional competence.

For humanitarian practice the operational lesson is unglamorous. Protection begins with the record: naming the dead as persons, counting them by name wherever possible, and preserving the chain that shows who authorised what. An institution that cannot produce its intermediate documents cannot be contradicted either, and an allegation that cannot be tested cannot be adjudicated. Testimony of this kind matters because it produces a version of events specific enough to be checked. The obligation it creates is not belief. It is inquiry.
